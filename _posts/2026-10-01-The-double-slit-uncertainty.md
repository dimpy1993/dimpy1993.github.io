The double-slit experiment is designed not to answer how nature works, but to demonstrate what happens when we probe it. An experiment is set up where a quantum particle, like a photon or electron, is fired from a single source and passes through two slits, with a detector on the other side to catch the result. When both slits are open, the detector captures an interference pattern, which is characteristic of wave nature. But when we open only one slit, the particles land as discrete packets, or quanta, forming a single clumped distribution rather than an alternating pattern. Naturally, the instinct is to see how the electrons are actually entering through the slits. If an electron is entering as a distinct packet, it must pass through either one of the holes, as a single localized packet cannot pass through both holes simultaneously. We set up a light source to observe the electron, but the moment we make that measurement, the distribution of electrons hitting the detector changes back into particle-like behavior, even with both slits open.
