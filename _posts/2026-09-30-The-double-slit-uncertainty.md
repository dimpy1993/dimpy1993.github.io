The double-slit experiment is designed not to answer how nature works, but to demonstrate what happens when we probe it. An experiment is set up where a quantum particle, like a photon or electron, is fired from a single source and passes through two slits, with a detector on the other side to catch the result. When both slits are open, the detector captures an interference pattern, which is characteristic of wave nature. But when we open only one slit, the particles land as discrete packets, or quanta, forming a single clumped distribution rather than an alternating pattern. Naturally, the instinct is to see how the electrons are actually entering through the slits. If an electron is entering as a distinct packet, it must pass through either one of the holes, as a single localized packet cannot pass through both holes simultaneously. We set up a light source to observe the electron, but the moment we make that measurement, the distribution of electrons hitting the detector changes back into particle-like behavior, even with both slits open.

Keep in mind certain caveats that is handwaved here such as the definition of a quantum measurement being vastly different from a classical one. It is clear that the very act of measurement destroys the wavelike behavior. As Feynman noted, summarizing Heisenberg, it is impossible to design an apparatus to determine which hole the electron passes through without disturbing it enough to destroy the interference pattern. By using light to fix the position of the electron, the probing photons transfer a random momentum kick to it, randomizing its phase and collapsing the wavefunction into a definitive localized distribution, leaving its momentum uncertain. This behavior transcends into the generalized version of quantum uncertainty that we learn in undergrad courses.

Before we make any measurement by installing a light source, the behavior of electrons passing the double-slit exhibits wave nature. It's not even that the electron has either a particle-like behavior or a wavelike behavior. Before making any measurement, such a question doesn't make sense quantum mechanically. Only after making the measurement can we fix our statement. The consensus is that before the measurement, the particle exists purely in a superposition of paths—effectively exploring all possibilities at once—which is described mathematically by a wavefunction. The square of this wavefunction gives us the actual probability of where it will land. In simpler terms, speaking as a layman, think of Schrödinger’s cat. Without opening the box, the cat is not definitively dead or alive; it exists in a quantum superposition of both states. We can know its true state only after making the measurement, which in this case means opening the box. The act of measurement fixes the state, an event we call wavefunction collapse. Or take another example: checking your weight on a weighing scale quantum mechanically. It's not about having a certain fixed quantity before you make a measurement; rather, the very act of measuring forces that quantity into reality.

The question isn't exactly why nature works in this way, because no one truly knows, it just is this way. The explanation we’ve just walked through, where the act of measurement forces the system to randomly pick a definitive state, is known as the Copenhagen interpretation. It relies on what we call "wavefunction collapse," and for decades, it has been the standard way physicists cope with these bizarre results. But because that sudden collapse feels so conceptually abrupt, other physicists prefer the Everett interpretation, more famously known as the _Many-Worlds_ theory. In Everett’s view, the wavefunction never actually collapses at all. Instead, it branches. When you open the box to check on Schrödinger’s cat, you don't force it to randomly become alive or dead; rather, reality itself splits. In one branch of the universe, you see a living cat, while in another newly created branch, a slightly different version of you is mourning a dead one.

This Everett branching is actually the foundation of my favorite Ted Chiang’s novella _Anxiety Is the Dizziness of Freedom_. Chiang explores a world where the Many-Worlds interpretation isn't just an abstract physical theory, but a tangible, accessible reality. In his universe, people possess devices that act as a window between timelines, allowing two recently split branches to temporarily interact and communicate. The story dives deep into the profound philosophical repercussions of such a situation. But whether reality collapses or branches, it still has nothing to do with a hidden property of the particle itself, and it's also not necessarily about our ignorance or lack of knowledge. It is just the intrinsic, probabilistic property of nature. The thing is, the math works—by abstracting it in some vector space called Hilbert space, we can make the calculation.
